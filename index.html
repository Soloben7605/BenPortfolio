Smart Glasses: Text Overlay Display

Overview

The idea started in a smart materials lecture on flexible transparent displays. I quickly found that a display on the lens can't work: the eye can't focus on anything 2 cm away. That turned the project into an optics problem first. The goal is lightweight glasses that show glanceable text (messages, directions, search results) with no camera and no speakers, keeping the power budget and the device privacy-first.

Key design decisions

  • 2D, head-locked overlay instead of 3D ARSimpler, less visually overwhelming, and enough for text.
  • Monochrome green micro-OLED (0.23″, 640×400, 10,000 nits)Green is where the eye is most sensitive, giving maximum brightness per watt and avoiding colour artefacts.
  • No cameraAvoids the social and battery costs of camera glasses. Microphone input is planned for future features such as live captioning.
  • Phone-tethered over Bluetooth Low EnergyThe glasses act as a low-power display while the phone does the heavy computation.
  • OpticsEvaluating a compact birdbath design (50/50 beamsplitter and concave mirror) against low-cost waveguide approaches, including research into 3D-printed geometric waveguides. Target field of view is roughly 15–20°.

Work so far

  • Researched accommodation, near point, eyebox, field of view and AR optical architectures, documented in a linked research vault.
  • Measured my own near point (8.8 ± 0.15 cm, giving an amplitude of accommodation of about 11.4 D) to quantify the problem: focusing on a display 2 cm away would need about 50 D, roughly 4.4 times my maximum.
  • Calculated the optical requirements: for a 15° field of view with a 5.06 mm-wide panel, the collimating optic needs a focal length of about 19 mm.
  • Selected the display and analysed its datasheet: single-lane MIPI DSI, power sequencing (1.8 V, then 5 V, then −2 V), and an I²C-controlled built-in test pattern for staged bring-up.

Next steps

  1. Build and test a 3D-printed optics mount with adjustable focus.
  2. Bring up the display on an ESP32-P4 development board.
  3. Design a custom PCB in stages: a display adapter board, then an integrated board, then a miniaturised wearable version.
